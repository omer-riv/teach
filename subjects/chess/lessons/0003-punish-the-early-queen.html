Chess Openings · Lesson 3 · ~10 min

Punish the early queen

You've stopped Scholar's mate. Now make your friend pay for trying it: one knight jump that often wins a rook.

Why this now. Lesson 2 got you to a safe position. But a friend who tries Scholar's mate usually keeps going with the queen. Every time you attack it, you get a free move. This lesson turns their favourite attack into a way for you to win.

Warm-up: from last time

1. e4 e5 2. Bc4 Nc6 3. Qh5. Your move?

g6 blocks the queen and attacks it. The other three allow Qxf7#.

Then 4. Qf3, aiming at f7 again. Your move?

Nf6 blocks the f-file and develops. That's the position this lesson starts from.

You're White in the Italian. Which move comes first?

e4, then Nf3, then Bc4. Keep it fresh: you'll need it as White.

A queen out early is a target

The queen is your most valuable piece, so when it's attacked it must move. If you attack it with a move you wanted to make anyway, such as developing a piece, you've gained a tempo: a move's worth of time. Your friend spends their move rescuing the queen while you keep developing. That's why the Scholar's mate attack is risky for White1.

The knight jump: …Nd4

After you've defended (g6, then Nf6), the queen is on f3. Friends often move it to b3, to aim at f7 once more. Here's the key move:

Why c2 matters: from c2 a knight would attack White's king on e1 and the rook on a1 at the same time. White has to save the queen and guard c2 with one move. A Lichess forum analysis recommends exactly this plan2, and the same ...Nd4 works against a queen on f3.

Watch it win a rook

  1. The usual start.
  2. You take the centre.
  3. The bishop aims at f7.
  4. Develop.
  5. Scholar's mate threat.
  6. Block and attack the queen. Lesson 2.
  7. Threatens mate again.
  8. Block. You've developed two knights; White has moved the queen three times.
  9. The queen goes round to aim at f7 once more (behind the bishop).
  10. The jump. It attacks the queen and eyes c2.
  11. The queen escapes and eyes your e5 pawn, but nothing guards c2 any more.
  12. Check, and the rook on a1 is attacked too: a fork.
  13. The king must move. (Kf1 or Ke2 changes nothing.)
  14. You've won a rook. That's usually enough to win a casual game.

If they check on f7: don't panic

Instead of moving the queen, White might grab a pawn with check: 6. Bxf7+. Your only move is Ke7. That looks scary, but your knight still attacks the queen. Once the queen moves away (say 7. Qd3), nothing protects the bishop, and your king takes it: 7… Kxf7. You've won a bishop for a pawn3.

When the trick isn't there

A careful friend plays 5. Ne2. That knight guards d4, so ...Nd4 just gets swapped off, and the engine says it would hand White the better game3. Before jumping, check: can White simply take the knight on d4? If so, don't. Develop instead: Bg7, then castle. You're already fine, because White's queen has wasted moves.

Queen on f3 or b3, and d4 unguarded? Jump: …Nd4.

Check yourself

1. White's queen goes to b3. Your best move?

Nd4 attacks the queen and eyes c2: the engine's clear top move.

2. Why is …Nd4 so strong there?

It attacks the queen, and threatens Nxc2+ forking king and rook. One White move can't easily fix both.

3. White played Qg3. Your move?

Nxc2+ forks the king on e1 and the rook on a1. Next move you take the rook.

4. White played Ne2. Should you jump to d4?

The knight on e2 can just take on d4. No trick: develop with Bg7 and castle.

5. After 6. Bxf7+ Ke7 7. Qd3, your move?

Nothing guards the bishop any more, so your king takes it. You're a piece up.

6. What is a tempo?

Attacking the queen with a developing move gains a tempo: your friend spends a move saving it.

Your win: you can defend Scholar's mate and then punish it, using …Nd4 to win material, and you know when the trick isn't there.

Try it for real: next time a friend brings the queen out early, defend, then look for a move that attacks it. And you still owe those three games as White with the Italian.
