Chess Openings · Lesson 2 · ~8 min

Don't get mated early

Your friend brings the queen out early, aiming at f7. Here's how to stop it in two moves and come out ahead.

Why this now. Scholar's mate is the most common way casual games end in under five moves. When you play Black, a friend will try it sooner or later. One defence handles every version of it.

Warm-up: from last time

The Italian setup's first three moves, in order:

e4, Nf3, Bc4. After that come c3, d3 and castling, in any order.

You're White in the Italian. A black knight lands on f6 before you've played d3. Now:

The knight attacks e4, so defend it with d3 first.

Three new symbols

+ means check. # means checkmate. ?? after a move means "a terrible mistake" (a blunder)3. And x means a capture: Qxf7 is "queen takes on f7".

In this lesson you're Black, so the boards are shown from your side: your pieces at the bottom, and the letters run h to a. The notation page has a drill for finding squares from either side.

The weak square: f7

At the start, the pawn on f7 is defended only by Black's king. Chess.com calls it weak for exactly that reason1. Two white pieces aimed at it, with nothing else guarding it, can mean checkmate. In lesson 1, your bishop on c4 aimed at f7 for the same reason.

Scholar's mate

  1. A normal start.
  2. You answer the same way.
  3. The bishop aims at f7.
  4. A good developing move.
  5. The queen joins in. Now two pieces hit f7, and only your king defends it. White threatens Qxf7#.
  6. A natural move: it attacks the queen. But it doesn't stop the threat.
  7. Checkmate. The bishop protects the queen, so your king can't take it, and it has no square to escape to1.

The defence: g6, then Nf6

Go back to the position after 3. Qh5. The move to play is 3… g6. It blocks the queen's path to f7, and it attacks the queen, so White has to spend a move saving it. Chess.com calls it the most sensible response1.

White often tries again with 4. Qf3, threatening Qxf7# along the f-file. Block it with 4… Nf6, which also develops a knight. Now f7 is safe, and you've brought out a piece while White has been moving the queen around2.

Queen hits f7: block it, and attack the queen if you can.

The trap inside the trap

Sometimes the queen comes out on move 2: 1. e4 e5 2. Qh5. Now don't play g6 yet. The queen also attacks your e5 pawn, and after 2… g6?? 3. Qxe5+ it checks your king and attacks your rook on h8 at the same time. That's a fork, and you lose the rook. Defend e5 first with 2… Nc6; Wikipedia calls it the cleanest defence2. After 3. Bc4, f7 is under threat again, so now g6 is right.

Why not do it yourself?

Against someone who knows the defence, an early queen just gets chased: g6 and Nf6 each develop with gain of time, and Black ends up ahead in development. Chess.com notes stronger players rarely try it for that reason1. Your Italian setup aims at f7 too, but slowly and safely, without putting the queen at risk.

The same weakness exists on f2 for White. In your Italian setup, castling puts your rook on f1, where it guards f2.

Check yourself

1. 1. e4 e5 2. Bc4 Nc6 3. Qh5. What does White threaten?

Queen and bishop both hit f7, which only the king defends: Qxf7#.

2. Same position. Your best move?

g6 blocks and attacks the queen. Qe7 also holds, but blocks your bishop. Nf6 and Bc5 lose to Qxf7#.

3. After 3… g6 4. Qf3, your move?

The queen now hits f7 along the f-file. Nf6 blocks it. The others allow Qxf7#.

4. Why is f7 weak early on?

Only the king defends f7, so two attackers can be enough.

5. 1. e4 e5 2. Qh5. Why is 2… g6 a blunder?

Qxe5+ forks your king and the rook on h8. Defend e5 with Nc6 first.

Your win: you can stop Scholar's mate in all three versions, and you know the one move order where g6 is a mistake.

Try it for real: you still owe three games as White with the Italian setup. When you play Black and the queen comes out early, look at f7.
