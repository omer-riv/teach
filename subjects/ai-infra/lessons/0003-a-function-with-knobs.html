Training AI Models · Lesson 3 · Part A · ~9 min

A function with knobs

Train a model by hand, then let gradient descent do it, and see what the learning rate does.

Why this first. Every way of training you'll meet, from pretraining to LoRA to RLHF, is this same loop at a bigger scale: measure how wrong the model is, then nudge its knobs to be less wrong. Get the small version into your hands and the big versions become variations on it.

A model is a function with knobs

Say you want to predict taxi fares from distance. A simple model:

fare = w × km

w is a knob: a number the model can adjust. A machine learning person would call it a parameter or weight, the same word as in lesson 1. Training means finding the knob values that make the model's predictions match real examples.

An LLM is the same idea: a function from text to "what comes next", with 70 billion knobs instead of one. Those are the 140 GB you sized in lesson 1.

Loss: one number for "how wrong"

To tune a knob you need a score. The loss is a single number measuring how wrong the predictions are on the training examples. Lower is better. A common one is mean squared error: for each example, take (prediction − actual)², then average2.

You just did training by hand. With one knob, dragging works. With 70 billion it doesn't, so we need the model to work out which way to turn each knob by itself.

The gradient: which way is downhill

The gradient says how the loss changes if you nudge the knob up a little. Its sign gives the direction: positive means turning w up makes the loss worse, so turn it down. Its size says how steep the slope is. Then take a step the other way1:

w ← w − learning rate × gradient

Repeat until the loss stops falling. That's gradient descent, the core of how neural networks learn1.

One step, with real numbers

  1. Start at w = 1. The loss is 10.7: way off.
  2. The gradient is −21.7. It's negative, so turning w up lowers the loss.
  3. With a learning rate of 0.04: w ← 1 − 0.04 × (−21.7) = 1.87.
  4. The new loss is 0.17. One step got most of the way there.

The learning rate: how big a step

The learning rate scales each step. It isn't learned; you choose it. A value you set before training is called a hyperparameter. Google's crash course puts it plainly: every model and dataset has its own ideal learning rate2. Try all three below, five steps each:

You should see three behaviours:

  • 0.005: too small. Steady progress, but slow. On a big model that means wasted GPU-hours.
  • 0.04: about right. Close to the best w in a couple of steps.
  • 0.1: too big. Every step overshoots further than the last, and the loss explodes. The training run diverges and never converges2.

When you later see a real training run's loss suddenly spike or go to NaN, the learning rate is the first suspect. Part C covers this.

Scaling up: billions of knobs

A real model needs a gradient for every knob at every step. Computing those one at a time would be hopeless. Backpropagation computes all of them in a single backward pass through the model, for roughly twice the cost of running the model forward. That's lesson 4. The update rule stays the same as the one you just used.

Practice

1. What does the loss measure?

One number for how far the predictions are from the real answers on the training examples. Training pushes it down.

2. The gradient for w is positive. To lower the loss, w should…

Positive gradient: raising w raises the loss. So step the other way: w − lr × gradient makes w smaller.

3. The loss jumps higher every step and blows up. The most likely cause?

Steps so big they overshoot the minimum, each further than the last. That's what 0.1 did in the lab.

4. Training an LLM means adjusting…

The parameters are the knobs. A 70B model has 70 billion of them, all nudged by gradient descent.

5. w = 2, gradient = 5, learning rate = 0.1. After one step, w is…

2 − 0.1 × 5 = 1.5.

Your win: you trained a model twice, by hand and by gradient descent, and you can say what the learning rate does when it's too small or too big.
